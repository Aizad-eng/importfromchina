Demurrage and Detention Charges Explained for Importers in Pakistan

By Muhammad Shamshad AslamUpdated November 12, 202610 min read
Quick answer

Demurrage and detention are daily charges for leaving cargo or a container too long. In Pakistan the port or terminal charges storage once its free period ends, and the shipping line charges detention for keeping its container past the free days. Both run on calendar days, so slow clearance or late paperwork quickly turns into cost.

What is the difference between demurrage and detention?

Demurrage, in everyday Pakistani usage, is the storage charge the port, terminal or airport cargo shed levies once goods stay beyond the free period. Detention is what the shipping line charges for keeping its container beyond the free time it allows. One is about space at the port, the other is about the line's equipment.

Maersk's customer advisory for Pakistan, effective 20 January 2025, puts it plainly: detention is a penalty for using a carrier's equipment while it is with the customer beyond the free time. The same advisory says the line's free time does not cover port storage or demurrage, which the consignee settles directly with the port. An importer can therefore owe two bills for the same delay.

When does the clock start?

For containers it usually starts at discharge. Maersk's advisory says free time for merchant haulage anywhere in Pakistan, and for carrier haulage to Karachi and Port Qasim, starts from the date the container is discharged at the port. For carrier haulage to upcountry dry ports it starts when the full container gates in at the dry port, though the line can still charge for time at the port if documents for moving the container were late.

Three details from the advisory matter for budgeting:

  • Free time and detention are counted in calendar days, including weekends.
  • Charges are set in US dollars and converted to rupees at the exchange rate on the day you pay.
  • Refrigerated containers carry a plug-in charge of USD 60 per day per unit.

How much free time do importers get?

It depends on the line, the container type and your contract. Each shipping line publishes its own Pakistan tariff, and larger importers often negotiate extra free days when they book. Read the free time on your own booking or contract rather than a general figure, and check whether it is counted from discharge or from gate-in at a dry port.

The port or terminal's free period for storage is separate from the line's free time. A container can still be inside the line's free days while port storage is already running, or the other way round, so ask your clearing agent to track both dates.

What customs deadlines feed into these charges?

Customs deadlines decide how long cargo sits, so they drive both bills. The Customs Act sets these:

  • Section 79: file the goods declaration within ten days of arrival. A declaration can be filed up to ten days before the vessel's expected arrival.
  • Section 82: penalties notified by the Board apply if the declaration is not filed within twenty days of arrival, or if goods are not removed within five days of clearance.
  • Section 82 proviso: goods become liable to confiscation if no declaration is filed within thirty days, and customs can move them towards auction after notice.
  • Section 83(2): duty not paid within ten days of assessment carries a surcharge of KIBOR plus three percent.

Missing NOCs are the other big cause. Goods that need a PSQCA release order, DRAP clearance or a PTA approval wait until it is filed, and under section 17 customs can detain goods that arrive without it.

Value disputes need not hold goods for long. Section 81 allows provisional assessment, where the importer pays or secures the disputed amount and the goods are released while customs settles the final figure, which stops storage building up during the argument.

Who pays when the delay is customs' fault?

Section 14A(2) of the Customs Act gives importers a remedy against port operators. Any agency or person running a customs port, customs airport, land customs station or container freight station must accept a delay and detention certificate issued by a customs officer not below the rank of Assistant Collector, and refund demurrage it received for delay that was not the importer's fault. A port operator that refuses to accept the certificate can face a penalty of up to five million rupees under clause 7A of section 156.

Note the scope. The section names port authorities and terminal and station operators, so it covers storage charged by them. Shipping line detention for the container is a separate commercial charge under your contract with the line.

Can customs penalties for late clearance be reduced?

Sometimes. Section 82 lets the Collector waive or reduce the late filing and late removal penalties where the delay came from circumstances beyond the importer's control. Ask your agent to apply in writing with evidence, such as a regulator's late release order, rather than paying and hoping for a refund later. Storage and detention are charged by the port and line, not customs, so a customs waiver does not cancel them; the delay and detention certificate under section 14A is the route for port storage.

Do airports charge storage too?

Yes. Air cargo sits in sheds run by terminal operators and ground handling agents, who allow a free period and then charge storage. Section 203 of the Customs Act lets the Collector fix the period after which goods left in a customs area become subject to storage fees. During the 2020 lockdown, Customs Today reported that FBR granted fifteen extra days free of demurrage and detention, and the Collector at Lahore's Allama Iqbal International Airport directed cargo terminal operators to apply it on top of their normal free time. How can an importer keep these charges down?

  1. File the declaration before arrival where the system allows it.
  2. Get NOCs and release orders lined up before the goods ship.
  3. Use the right PCT code so the declaration is not reassessed.
  4. Pay duty quickly once assessed.
  5. Ask for a delay and detention certificate if customs caused the delay, and claim the refund from the port operator.
  6. Return empty containers promptly and keep proof of the gate-in date, including the dry port gate-in record for carrier haulage upcountry, since that date starts the line's free time there.
  7. Negotiate free time with the line when booking if your goods often take longer to clear.

A good clearing agent handles most of this. Our guide on what a customs clearing agent does explains how. Do not plan around an official waiver; the 2020 relief was a one-off for a national emergency.

Do single-item buyers pay demurrage?

Not usually in the container sense, because one item travels by courier or consolidated air freight rather than in its own container.
