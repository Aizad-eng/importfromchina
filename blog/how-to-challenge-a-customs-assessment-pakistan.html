How to Challenge a Customs Assessment in Pakistan

By Muhammad Shamshad AslamUpdated November 12, 202611 min read
Quick answer

You challenge a customs assessment in stages. First answer the reassessment notice and ask for a hearing. Then appeal to the Collector (Appeals) within 30 days, then to the Customs Appellate Tribunal within 45 days, and finally refer a question of law to the High Court within 30 days. Valuation rulings have their own 30-day review route, and ADR is an option.

What counts as a customs assessment?

An assessment is customs' decision on how much duty and tax your goods owe. The Customs Act 1969 defines it to include a provisional assessment, a reassessment and any order where the duty assessed is nil. Most commercial imports start with self-assessment: under section 79 the importer, usually through a clearing agent, declares the goods and works out the duty. Under section 80, customs checks that declaration and reassesses the goods if anything in it is wrong.

Disputes usually turn on three things: the value, the PCT code, or whether a concession applies. For how duty is built up in the first place, see our guide on customs duty from China to Pakistan, and for the wider process, how to import from China to Pakistan.

What can you do before the goods are released?

Act at the reassessment stage, because it is the cheapest point to win:

  • Ask for a hearing. Section 80(3) says that when customs reassesses goods, a notice is served through the customs computer system and an opportunity of hearing is given if you ask for it. Bring the invoice, payment proof, the seller's specification and anything else that supports your value or code.
  • Use provisional assessment. Where customs needs a laboratory check or more inquiry, section 81 lets an officer not below Assistant Collector assess provisionally. You pay the extra amount, or secure it with a bank guarantee or pay order and an indemnity bond, and the goods are released. The final figure must be set within ninety days, extendable by thirty in exceptional cases, and if it is not, the provisional assessment becomes final. Provisional valuation is not allowed where a valuation ruling is in force.
  • Correct clerical errors. Section 155R lets an importer ask an Assistant Collector or above for a correction certificate for clerical or typing mistakes in the declaration.
  • Claim abatement for damage. Section 27 allows a reduction in duty for goods damaged before or during unloading, if this is reported before examination.

How do you appeal to the Collector (Appeals)?

This is the first formal appeal for most assessments. Under section 193, anyone aggrieved by a decision or order under sections 33, 79, 80, 131, 179 or 195 passed by an officer below the rank of Additional Collector may appeal to the Collector (Appeals). The key points:

  • Deadline: thirty days from the date the decision is communicated to you. A late appeal can be admitted if the Collector (Appeals) accepts there was sufficient cause.
  • Fee: one thousand rupees.
  • Hearing: you have a right to be heard, and you can raise a ground not in the appeal if leaving it out was not wilful or unreasonable.
  • Stay: the Collector (Appeals) can stay recovery of duty and taxes for up to thirty days after hearing the department.
  • Decision time: within ninety days of filing, extendable by up to sixty days, with reasons in writing.

When does the case go to the Customs Appellate Tribunal?

Section 194A sends four kinds of order to the Appellate Tribunal: decisions by an officer of Additional Collector rank or above under section 179, orders of the Collector (Appeals), orders under section 195, and revision orders of the Director General of Customs Valuation under section 25D.

  • Deadline: forty-five days from communication of the order, with late appeals admitted for sufficient cause.
  • Fee: twenty thousand rupees for a company and five thousand rupees for others.
  • Payment: the assessed duty stays payable unless the Tribunal stays recovery. A stay can run for thirty days and be extended up to ninety days, and is subject to a pay order or bank guarantee of at least twenty-five percent of the principal amount.
  • Decision: within ninety days of filing, extendable by sixty with both parties' consent. The Tribunal does not remand cases, and at the first hearing it must offer the appellant alternative dispute resolution.
  • Small cases: the Tribunal may refuse to admit an appeal where the amount involved does not exceed fifty thousand rupees.

Can you take the case to the High Court?

Only on a question of law, or a mixed question of law and fact. Under section 196, the aggrieved person or an authorised customs officer may file a reference within thirty days of receiving the Tribunal's order. The fee for a person other than customs is fifty thousand rupees, a special bench hears it, and the Act asks for a decision within six months. Duty remains payable under the Tribunal's order unless the High Court stays recovery, and such a stay also needs a pay order or bank guarantee of at least twenty-five percent.

Section 217(2) bars a civil suit to set aside or modify an assessment, so the statutory appeal route is the way to go.

How do you challenge a valuation ruling instead?

If your goods were assessed on a value fixed by a valuation ruling, the route is a review petition under section 25D to the Director General of Customs Valuation, filed within thirty days of the determination. The proceedings must be completed within sixty days of filing. FBR's valuation page describes section 25D revision as the statutory remedy for anyone aggrieved by a notified value. An order passed on review can then go to the Appellate Tribunal. Our guide on how customs valuation rulings work explains the rulings themselves.

Is there a faster route than appeals?

Two options can shorten the fight:

  • Alternative dispute resolution (ADR). Under section 195C, a person with a dispute pending before a court or appellate authority may apply to FBR for a committee, with a proposal and an offer of payment. FBR appoints a committee within fifteen days, including a retired High Court judge, which decides within forty-five days, extendable by fifteen. Recovery is stayed while it works, and its decision binds the Collector. Criminal cases and points of law with wider revenue impact are excluded.
  • Revision by customs itself. Section 195 lets the Board, a Chief Collector, a Director General or a Collector examine a subordinate officer's decision and pass fresh orders, within two years of the decision.

For future imports, an advance ruling under section 212B can settle a classification or origin question before you import. It is binding on customs for three years unless the law or facts change.

How do you get a refund if you overpaid?

Under section 33, a refund of duty paid through inadvertence, error or misconstruction must be claimed within one year of payment. For a provisional assessment the year runs from the final assessment, and where a refund follows a decision of an officer, the Tribunal or a court, it runs from that decision. Claims should be decided within one hundred and twenty days, extendable by ninety. A refund is refused if the duty has been passed on to the buyer.

What mistakes weaken a challenge?

  • Missing the deadline. Late filing needs a good reason and is never guaranteed.
  • Skipping the reassessment hearing. It is the easiest place to fix a value or code.
  • No proof of payment. An invoice without a bank record of what you paid is weak evidence of value.
  • A declaration you cannot defend. If the original value was understated, an appeal can expose penalties under section 32. Since December 2024 clearing agents also lose licence points for misdeclaration, so a careful agent will not file a value you cannot support.
  • Not budgeting for the twenty-five percent security needed to stay recovery at the Tribunal or High Court.

What can you do about a courier parcel assessment?

For a courier parcel, the courier files the declaration and receives the assessment, so start with the courier. Ask for a copy of the assessment, the value and code used, and send your invoice and payment proof for them to put to customs. Formal appeals are rarely worth it for a small parcel, but the same routes exist. Our guide on a parcel stuck at customs covers that situation.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to appeal a customs assessment?

Thirty days to the Collector (Appeals) for orders by officers below Additional Collector, then forty-five days to the Appellate Tribunal and thirty days for a High Court reference.

What is the fee to appeal to the Collector (Appeals)?

One thousand rupees under section 193 of the Customs Act.

Do I have to pay the duty while my appeal is pending?

Yes, unless recovery is stayed. Stays at the Tribunal and High Court need a pay order or bank guarantee of at least twenty-five percent of the principal amount.

Can I get my goods released while customs checks the value?

Often yes. Section 81 allows provisional assessment, where you pay or secure the difference and the goods are released while the final figure is worked out within ninety days.

How do I challenge a valuation ruling?

File a review petition under section 25D with the Director General of Customs Valuation within thirty days. The proceedings must finish within sixty days.

How long do I have to claim a customs refund?

One year from payment under section 33, or from the final assessment or the deciding order where those apply.
